Pyrite (FeS₂)

Pyrite (FeS&sub2;) is an iron disulfide mineral — the most abundant sulfide mineral in the Earth’s crust. Its pale brass-yellow metallic luster has earned it the informal name “fool’s gold.” German: Pyrit. Despite being grouped with “sulfide minerals,” pyrite belongs to the disulfide compound class, not the simple sulfide class.

Pyrite as Iron Disulfide

The formula FeS&sub2; indicates one iron(II) ion per disulfide anion (S&sub2;²¯). The two sulfur atoms in pyrite are bonded to each other (S–S bond), making this formally an iron disulfide rather than an iron sulfide. This is different from pyrrhotite (FeS), which is iron(II) sulfide with a simple S²¯ anion.

Pyrite in the Mineral Family

Pyrite belongs to the pyrite mineral group, which follows the general formula MX&sub2; where M is a transition metal and X is sulfur or other chalcogen. Related minerals include marcasite (the orthorhombic polymorph of FeS&sub2;). Pyrite occurs in a wide range of geological environments: sedimentary, metamorphic, and hydrothermal.

Fool’s Gold and Compound Class Mapping

The nickname “fool’s gold” comes from pyrite being visually similar to gold but being iron sulfide rather than the precious metal. In the context of the Bisulfid Atlas, pyrite serves as a concrete example of the disulfide compound class in mineral form, distinct from the sulfide minerals (like sphalerite ZnS or galena PbS).
