Hydroxid

Hydroxid is the German-form name for what English calls hydroxide. German drops the final E from the suffix: hydroxide (EN) becomes Hydroxid (DE). This follows the same -id/-ide pattern as Bisulfid/bisulfide, Sulfid/sulfide, and Oxid/oxide.

What Hydroxid Names

Hydroxide compounds contain the OH¹¯ ion — one oxygen and one hydrogen bonded together, carrying a single negative charge. Common examples include sodium hydroxide (Natriumhydroxid, NaOH), potassium hydroxide (Kaliumhydroxid, KOH), and calcium hydroxide (Calciumhydroxid, Ca(OH)&sub2;). German names for all of these use the -id suffix: Hydroxid.

Hydroxid in the -id Pattern

Hydroxid illustrates an important feature of the -id suffix pattern: it applies not just to monatomic anions (like S²¯ or Cl¹¯) but also to the polyatomic hydroxide ion (OH¹¯). The suffix marks the compound class regardless of whether the anion is monatomic. See German Chemical Suffix for the full scope.

Hydroxid vs Oxid

Oxid (German: oxide, O²¯) and Hydroxid (German: hydroxide, OH¹¯) are distinct compound classes. Oxid compounds contain the bare oxide dianion; Hydroxid compounds contain the hydroxide anion with its hydrogen. Both follow the -id suffix convention in German.
