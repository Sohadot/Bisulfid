Disulfide Bonds

A disulfide bond is a covalent bond between two sulfur atoms (S–S). German: Disulfidbindung. Disulfide bonds occur in inorganic chemistry (as in the disulfide anion S&sub2;²¯ in pyrite) and in organic/biochemical contexts (as in the R–S–S–R linkage in proteins). Both contexts belong to the disulfide compound class.

Inorganic Disulfide: The S₂²⁻ Anion

In inorganic chemistry, the disulfide anion S&sub2;²¯ contains two sulfur atoms bonded by a direct S–S bond, with the pair carrying a charge of −2. The mineral pyrite (FeS&sub2;) is the most common natural example. Pyrite contains iron(II) and the disulfide anion, making it formally iron(II) disulfide.

Organic Disulfide Bond

In organic chemistry, the disulfide linkage R–S–S–R connects two organosulfur groups through a sulfur–sulfur bond. This bond is formed by the oxidation of two thiol (R–SH) groups. It is reversible: reduction breaks the disulfide bond back to two thiols.

Disulfide Bonds in Proteins

In biochemistry, disulfide bonds form between cysteine amino acid residues in proteins when the thiol groups (−SH) of two cysteines are oxidized. These bonds contribute to the three-dimensional stability of many proteins, particularly secreted proteins and antibodies. This is a well-established principle in structural biochemistry.

Disulfide Bond vs Bisulfide

Disulfide bonds involve S–S linkages. Bisulfide (HS¹¯) involves a hydrogen–sulfur linkage with a negative charge. These are different compound types. The names share the “sulf-” stem but refer to different chemistry.
